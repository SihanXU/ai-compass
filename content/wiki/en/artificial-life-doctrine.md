---
summary: "Intelligence is a late product of life-like processes, so we should grow it from self-organising, self-replicating systems rather than train it."
---
## Beliefs

The Artificial-Life Doctrine thinks the field started at the wrong end. Brains are not where intelligence begins; they are what billions of years of self-maintenance, reproduction and adaptation eventually produced. So instead of fitting a giant function to a static dataset, it studies cellular automata that grow and repair shapes, digital organisms that compete for CPU time, and soft simulated creatures that must keep themselves alive. Get the life-like dynamics right, it argues, and cognition will be a byproduct you did not have to design.

It sits low and far toward the World side: intelligence is rooted in bodies, metabolism and environments, not in symbols. Its Harness position reflects a preference for many interacting local units over one central model; there is no master network, only rules that every cell follows. Its dark-revolutionary shade is earned honestly, since it rejects not just the architecture of current AI but the whole train-on-a-dataset framing.

## Arguments

- Neural cellular automata trained with purely local update rules learn to grow a target pattern and regenerate it after damage, showing robust global behaviour from local computation.
- Self-repair, open-ended adaptation and robustness to novel conditions are exactly where today's large models are weakest, and they are life's specialities.
- Life is the only process known to have produced general intelligence, so studying its dynamics directly is the most evidence-based route available.

## Critiques

- Decades of artificial-life research have produced fascinating toys but no system with competitive performance on any practical cognitive task.
- Evolution took billions of years on a planet-sized computer; there is no clear argument that simulated life can compress that timescale enough to matter.
- "Life-like" is a slippery success criterion, which makes results hard to compare and easy to over-interpret.
