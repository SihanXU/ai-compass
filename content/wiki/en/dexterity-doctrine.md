summary: "Holds that dexterous manipulation with multi-fingered hands is the true test and engine of physical intelligence."
---
## Beliefs

Dexterity Doctrine believes intelligence lives in the hands. Walking is impressive, but many animals walk; what sets humans apart is the ability to twirl a pen, tie a knot, crack an egg or turn a key. These tasks demand fine contact control, tactile sensing, coordination across many degrees of freedom and constant adjustment to slips and surprises. A robot that can do them has solved most of what makes physical work hard, and the effort to get there forces progress in perception, control and learning all at once.

It sits at the far World edge and in the middle band: dexterity typically needs a learned policy wrapped in careful hardware, sensing and control engineering, neither a pure monolith nor a loose agent system. Its paradigm level is progressive. It insists that grippers and pick-and-place are not enough, and that the frontier of robotics is the multi-fingered hand.

## Arguments

- Reinforcement learning in simulation with heavy randomization has produced in-hand manipulation on real multi-fingered robot hands, including reorienting objects and solving a Rubik's cube.
- Combining demonstrations with reinforcement learning makes high-dimensional hand control tractable for complex tool-use tasks.
- Human environments are designed for human hands; a capable hand unlocks tools and objects that simple grippers cannot use.

## Critiques

- Multi-fingered hands are expensive, fragile and hard to maintain, and many industrial tasks are solved well by simpler grippers.
- Tactile sensing hardware remains immature, limiting how much dexterity can be learned from vision alone.
- Generalist-policy camps argue dexterity will come as a byproduct of scaling general robot data, not as its own doctrine.
