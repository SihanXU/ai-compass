## Beliefs

Humanoid Centralism starts from an architectural observation: stairs, door handles, kitchens, tools and vehicles were all designed for creatures with two legs, two arms and two five-fingered hands at roughly human height. A robot shaped like a person can, in principle, use all of that infrastructure without anyone rebuilding it. Equally important, the largest source of manipulation data is humans themselves, and a humanoid body makes that data far easier to transfer.

It sits on the World side near the top of the chart. The World lean is strong: the doctrine is about bodies in physical space. The Monolith lean reflects its preference for one standard form factor running one generalist policy, rather than a zoo of task-specific machines. Its paradigm level is orthodox. Humanoids are old as an aspiration, and the bet that they are the right vehicle for learned general intelligence now runs with the mainstream rather than against it.

## Arguments

- A human-shaped robot can operate in unmodified homes, factories and offices built around human ergonomics.
- Human video and teleoperation map naturally onto humanoid kinematics, making imitation learning from people more direct.
- Standardizing on one form factor concentrates data, software and manufacturing investment instead of fragmenting it.

## Critiques

- Bipedal humanoids are mechanically complex, energy-hungry and harder to make safe than wheeled or fixed platforms.
- Many valuable tasks are better served by specialized bodies, from warehouse carts to surgical arms.
- Morphology-first rivals argue that copying the human shape is aesthetic conservatism rather than an engineering optimum.
