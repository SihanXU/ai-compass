summary: "Robot bodies, sensors and datasets should be shared openly so that embodied AI is built as a commons, not a moat."
---
## Beliefs

Open-Hardware Mutualism extends the open-source bargain from code and weights to atoms. CAD files, wiring diagrams, firmware, calibration procedures and the datasets recorded on the hardware should all be published, so that any lab can rebuild a robot, reproduce a result and contribute data back to a shared pool. The mutualism is practical rather than utopian: robot data is so expensive to collect that no single group can gather enough of it, and pooling across institutions is the only way most of them get a seat at the table.

It sits on the World side of the chart, because its whole concern is physical embodiment, and toward the Harness end, because it imagines progress as an ecosystem of interoperable parts, data formats and community standards rather than one company's vertically integrated robot. Its shade is neutral: the paradigm is ordinary robot learning; the radical part is the licence.

## Arguments

- Cross-institution datasets that merge demonstrations from many different robot embodiments have shown that pooled data can train policies that beat models trained on a single lab's robot.
- Reproducibility in robotics is notoriously poor, and an open bill of materials is the only way a reviewer can rebuild the setup that produced a result.
- Shared hardware standards cut the time each new lab spends reinventing grippers, mounts and calibration, freeing effort for actual research.

## Critiques

- Open designs rarely receive the industrial engineering, certification and supply-chain work that make hardware reliable and safe at scale.
- Pooled datasets inherit every inconsistency of their contributors: different cameras, action spaces and labelling conventions make "more data" harder to use than it sounds.
- Funding for hardware is scarce, and a commons with no business model can stall once the grant that launched it ends.
